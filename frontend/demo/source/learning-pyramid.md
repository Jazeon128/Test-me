# The learning pyramid: a famous chart and what the evidence says

## The chart

Few diagrams in education travel as far as the learning pyramid. It usually appears as a triangle split into seven bands. Each band names a way of learning and gives the share of material people supposedly remember afterwards. The common version reads like this:

| Activity | Claimed average retention |
|---|---|
| Lecture | 5% |
| Reading | 10% |
| Audio-visual | 20% |
| Demonstration | 30% |
| Discussion | 50% |
| Practice by doing | 75% |
| Teaching others | 90% |

The lesson people draw is simple. Passive methods sit at the narrow top and fail. Active methods sit at the wide base and work. Teaching others is presented as the most powerful method of all.

The chart appears in teacher training courses, corporate slide decks, medical education papers and university websites. It is often labelled with the name of a respected source. The problem is that nobody has been able to find the research behind the numbers.

## Where the numbers are said to come from

The seven-band version is most often credited to the NTL Institute for Applied Behavioral Science in Bethel, Maine. When the researcher Kåre Letrud asked NTL for the underlying study, the institute replied in October 2009. It said it had developed and used the pyramid in the early 1960s, when it was part of the National Education Association's adult education division. It said it still believed the figures were accurate. It also said it could no longer locate the original research. Letrud published this exchange in 2012 in the journal *Education*.

That reply matters. A retention rate is a measurement. To mean anything, it needs a defined group of learners, defined material, a defined test and a defined delay between learning and testing. None of these details has ever been produced for the seven figures.

## Edgar Dale and the Cone of Experience

The second name often printed on the chart is Edgar Dale. Dale was an American educator. In 1946 he published *Audio-Visual Methods in Teaching*, which introduced a diagram he called the Cone of Experience.

Dale's cone arranged kinds of experience by how concrete or abstract they are. Direct, purposeful experience sat at the base. Verbal symbols, such as words on a page, sat at the top. Between them came demonstrations, field trips, exhibits, film, radio and pictures.

Dale's book settles three questions about the original cone:

1. **It contains no percentages.** The diagram on page 39 has no retention figures of any kind.
2. **It is not a ranking of teaching methods.** Dale described the cone as a visual metaphor. In a section titled "What the Cone Is and Is Not", he warned readers not to treat it as a hierarchy in which lower bands are better.
3. **Learners do not have to climb it.** Dale also rejected the idea that a learner must move from the base to the top in order.

So the chart that carries Dale's name ranks teaching methods by retention, which is exactly the reading Dale's own book rejects.

## How the numbers got attached to the cone

Historians of the chart describe an overlay. A list of retention percentages circulated on its own for decades. At some point it was printed on top of Dale's cone, and the list borrowed Dale's authority.

Retention percentages were circulating 40 years before Dale drew his cone:

- **1906.** Letrud and Hernes trace a quantified claim back to Charles Roads, writing in a Sunday school magazine. His fractions work out to about 10% for hearing, 50% for seeing, 70% for saying and 90% for doing.
- **1967.** D. G. Treichler printed a list in a film and audio-visual trade magazine: 10% of what people read, 20% of what they hear, 30% of what they see, 50% of what they see and hear, 70% of what they say, and 90% of what they say while doing. Treichler named no studies.
- **1977.** Ann Bauman's training manual is the earliest clearly documented case of percentages printed on a cone, according to a 2014 audit by Subramony, Molenda, Betrus and Thalheimer.
- **1978.** A United States government workbook for alcoholism trainers printed recall percentages beside a cone credited to Dale.

The exact first appearance of the seven-band 5 to 90 version has not been established. Different lists have also circulated side by side, which is itself a warning sign. Measured data does not change shape each time it is copied.

## The critics

Researchers have taken the chart apart from several directions.

- **Will Thalheimer, 2006.** Thalheimer followed one citation to a published research article. The article did not contain the graph. The researcher named as its source told him she did not recognise it.
- **Lalley and Miller, 2007.** They found no credible research supporting the chart's specific rates. They concluded that the best method depends on the task and the learners.
- **Letrud, 2012.** Besides NTL's missing study, Letrud showed that the categories are poorly defined. "Audio-visual" and "demonstration" overlap, for example, and no test conditions are given.
- **Kevin Masters, 2013 and 2020.** Masters reviewed 43 medical education publications that used the pyramid, then 41 more in a follow-up. Many cited unsuitable, missing or circular sources. Some even cited the critiques as if they supported the chart.
- **Letrud and Hernes, 2016 and 2018.** They found the myth spreading through academic journals and traced many changing versions back over a century.

An honest summary is this: no identifiable empirical study has been found that measured the seven figures. Failing to find a study does not prove one never existed. It does mean nobody should present the numbers as established fact.

## What the research does support

The pyramid's numbers are unsupported, but some of its spirit is not. Several well-tested findings point in a similar direction. Each comes from a specific comparison under specific conditions, so none of them produces a universal retention percentage.

### Retrieval practice

Roediger and Karpicke (2006) had students read short prose passages. Some students then took a recall test. Others restudied the passage instead. On a test five minutes later, restudying won: 81% recalled versus 75%. One week later the pattern reversed. The students who had been tested recalled 56% of the material. The students who had restudied recalled 42%. The effect size was d = 0.83.

The lesson is that the method which feels best right away is not always the one that lasts. Testing yourself is harder than rereading, and it pays off later.

### Learning by teaching

Fiorella and Mayer (2013) compared students who studied and then explained the material on video with students who studied for a test. One week later, the students who had taught did better, with an effect size of d = 0.79. Students who only prepared to teach, without actually teaching, showed a smaller difference that was not statistically significant.

Nestojko and colleagues (2014) found a related effect. Students who merely expected to teach the material recalled more of it than students who expected a test, with d = 0.56, even though they never taught.

### Active learning in science courses

Freeman and colleagues (2014) combined 225 studies of undergraduate science, engineering and mathematics courses. Exam scores were 0.47 standard deviations higher under active learning than under traditional lecturing. Failure rates were 21.8% with active learning and 33.8% with lecturing. Note that this measures exam performance and course failure, not a percentage of material remembered.

### The generation effect

Slamecka and Graf (1978) showed that people remember words they generate themselves better than words they simply read. A 2007 meta-analysis by Bertsch and colleagues, covering 86 studies, put the average advantage at 0.40 standard deviations.

## A related myth: learning styles

The pyramid sometimes travels with labels such as visual, auditory and kinaesthetic learners. The claim is that teaching to a person's preferred style improves learning. Pashler and colleagues (2008) reviewed the evidence and found it insufficient to support that matching prescription. People do have preferences. What lacks support is the claim that matching instruction to them improves results.

## How to use this knowledge

1. **Do not quote the percentages.** If you see the chart, ask what study produced the numbers, who the learners were, and when they were tested.
2. **Keep the useful idea.** Practising retrieval, explaining material to others and generating answers all have real evidence behind them.
3. **Watch for delayed effects.** Rereading feels effective in the moment. Self-testing usually wins a week later.
4. **Judge methods by fit.** The right method depends on the material, the learners and the goal. No fixed ladder of methods replaces that judgment.
